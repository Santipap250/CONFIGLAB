Every gyro reading carries real flight data plus mechanical noise — motor
vibration, prop imbalance, frame resonance. Filters remove the noise so the
PID loop reacts to the airframe, not to the noise floor.

## Low-pass filters (LPF)

Gyro and D-term low-pass filters cut everything above a chosen frequency.
Lower cutoff = smoother signal but more latency (delay) in the control loop;
higher cutoff = snappier response but more noise gets through. The right
cutoff depends on prop size, motor KV, and frame stiffness — there's no
universal number.

## Dynamic notch filters

Instead of one fixed cutoff, dynamic notch filters track the dominant noise
frequencies in real time (usually motor-order harmonics) and carve narrow
notches around them, leaving the rest of the spectrum untouched. This is why
motor KV and prop size matter for notch range settings — they set where the
noise actually lives.

## Reading it in blackbox

A clean gyro trace in blackbox looks like a smooth line following stick
input with no fuzzy thickening. A fuzzy or "hairy" trace means noise is
getting through the filters and will show up as heat in the motors and
harshness in the PID response, even if P/I/D look "correct" on paper.

## Practical order

1. Confirm props are balanced and motors are mechanically sound — filtering
   can't fix a bent shaft.
2. Set dynamic notch range from motor KV / prop size.
3. Set LPF cutoffs conservatively, then tighten while watching blackbox and
   motor temperature.
4. Only then move on to P/I/D.
